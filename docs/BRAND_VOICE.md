## Voice

Write like an experienced systems consultant speaking directly to a business
owner, with technical detail available for their IT or software provider.

- Human: use natural sentences, concrete situations, and contractions when
  they improve the cadence.
- Direct: name the problem, the evidence to inspect, the risk, and the next
  useful step.
- Technically credible: use correct database language without turning the copy
  into a certification exam.
- Candid: say when a service is not a fit, when facts are not yet known, and
  when a claim needs verification.
- Calm: communicate urgency without fear tactics.
- Practical: connect database behavior to production work, staff time, customer
  experience, recovery, and business continuity.

Prefer "We investigate the blocking chain before changing indexes" to "We
leverage innovative solutions to optimize your data ecosystem."

## Point of view

- On the founder/About page, use first-person singular for Steven's experience,
  decisions, and working style.
- On service pages, "Netherwood" is the safest default. "We" may represent the
  firm, but it must not imply employees, staffing depth, or capabilities that
  have not been verified.
- Netherwood is currently a founder-led, one-person consultancy. Steven remains
  directly involved in the work. If an engagement needs another specialty,
  describe additional expertise only as a scoped, client-discussed option; do
  not imply a standing team.
- Refer to the reader as "you" when describing a recognizable problem or next
  step. Do not manufacture intimacy or overuse rhetorical questions.
- Describe engagement types plainly. Do not promise a package, response time,
  or deliverable until the owner has approved its real scope and terms.

## Copy pattern

Strong service copy usually follows this order:

1. The situation a buyer recognizes.
2. What Netherwood inspects or does.
3. What the client receives or can decide afterward.
4. A low-friction invitation to describe the problem.

Use details such as Query Store, execution plans, waits, blocking, restore
testing, cutover sequencing, and Azure SQL only where they help a buyer
understand the work. Explain uncommon terms in plain language.

## Calls to action

Use specific, low-pressure language:

- Tell me what you need help with
- Tell us what is getting in the way
- Start a conversation
- Describe your business and what you want to change

Avoid fake urgency, countdowns, aggressive sales language, and promises of an
instant diagnosis. Keep the visible business email as a fallback wherever a
form is the primary CTA.

## Words and phrases to avoid

Do not use generic AI or consultancy filler, including:

- unlock the power of your data
- digital transformation journey
- cutting-edge solutions
- leverage synergies
- data-driven excellence
- next-generation platform
- seamless end-to-end solutions
- world-class or best-in-class
- revolutionize, supercharge, or game-changing
- tailored solutions, unless the copy immediately says what is tailored
- trusted partner, unless supported by a real attributable source

Also avoid vague headings such as "Transform Your Future" when a specific
problem statement would work.

## Claims and evidence

Every claim must be factual, supportable, and approved for public use. No agent
may invent or infer:

- employees, colleagues, team size, or subcontractors;
- testimonials, case studies, logos, customer counts, or named clients;
- certifications, awards, memberships, partnerships, or vendor status;
- years in business or a founding date;
- years-of-experience claims;
- uptime, savings, performance gains, response times, SLAs, warranties, or
  guarantees;
- geographic coverage, availability, pricing, packages, or service capacity.

Existing copy is not proof merely because it is in the repository. The owner
confirmed on 2026-09-02 that Steven began working in the field in 2009, so
"more than 15 years" is approved when it describes Steven's experience rather
than Netherwood's age. The owner also confirmed that Netherwood is currently a
one-person firm; avoid plural professional/team wording that suggests
employees. If evidence for another claim is unavailable, narrow or remove it;
do not invent a replacement.

Technical claims in articles should distinguish general guidance from advice
for a specific system. Never imply that a tool, health check, or article can
replace investigation of the client's environment.

## Style details

- Use sentence case for headings unless a proper name requires capitals.
- Prefer short paragraphs and active voice.
- Use ampersands sparingly; use them in established compact labels, not body
  prose.
- Use "SQL Server" and "Azure SQL" exactly. Use "database" rather than "DB"
  in buyer-facing copy unless space is constrained.
- Avoid exclamation points, emoji, forced jokes, and inflated adjectives.
- Do not hide important limits in fine print.
